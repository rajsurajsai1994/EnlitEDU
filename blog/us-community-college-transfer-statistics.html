Community College Transfer Statistics: What U.S. Data Shows

Millions of students start at a U.S. community college planning to transfer. National data shows where that path breaks down, and much of it runs through the registrar’s office.

October 16, 2026 8 min read EnlitEDU Team · Reviewed by Chaitanya Saripalli
EnlitEDU TeamWritten with registrars, evaluators and enrollment leaders. Updated 2026-10-16.
Reviewed by Chaitanya SaripalliFounder & CEO of EnlitEDU and founder of Anatta IT Solutions, which supports colleges’ student information systems. LinkedIn

Key takeaways

  • U.S. community colleges enrolled about 10.5 million students in fall 2024, including 6.4 million in credit courses.
  • Fewer than a third of students who start at a community college transfer to a four-year institution within six years (31.6% of the fall 2017 cohort).
  • About half of those who transfer earn a bachelor’s degree within six years of starting, so roughly one in six community college starters gets there.
  • GAO estimated that students who transferred lost about 43% of their credits on average. The loss was lowest, about 22%, from public two-year to public four-year colleges.
  • Slow or inconsistent credit evaluation is one of the few parts of this problem a college controls directly.

Community colleges are the front door to American higher education for a large share of students, and transfer is supposed to be the hallway to a bachelor’s degree. National data from the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center, the U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO), the American Association of Community Colleges (AACC) and the Community College Research Center (CCRC) shows that the hallway has gaps. Here are the numbers registrars, transfer advisors and enrollment leaders should know, and what they mean in practice.

How many students attend U.S. community colleges?

According to AACC’s Fast Facts 2025, about 10.5 million students attended community colleges in fall 2024: roughly 6.4 million in credit programs and 4.1 million in non-credit programs. Headcount rose 3.9% from fall 2023. A growing share are still in high school: CCRC reports that dual enrollment students made up 22% of community college enrollment in 2023-24 (see our guide to evaluating dual enrollment credit).

What percentage of community college students transfer?

The Clearinghouse’s Tracking Transfer series follows first-time students who start at a community college. For the fall 2017 starting cohort:

Measure (fall 2017 community college starters)Result
Transferred to a four-year institution within six years31.6%
Of those who transferred, earned a bachelor’s degree49.7%
Share of all starters who earned a bachelor’s (calculated)about 15.7%, roughly one in six

Those figures have barely moved across recent cohorts. The original Tracking Transfer study, led by CCRC with the Aspen Institute and the Clearinghouse, reported the same pattern: about a third of community college starters transfer, and about 16% earn a bachelor’s degree within six years.

How much credit do transfer students lose?

GAO’s 2017 report Students Need More Information to Help Reduce Challenges in Transferring College Credits (GAO-17-574) analyzed students who transferred between 2004 and 2009. It estimated they lost, on average, about 43% of their credits. The path mattered:

  • Public two-year to public four-year, the most common route, had the lowest loss: about 22%.
  • Public to public transfers overall lost about 37%.
  • Private for-profit to public transfers lost about 94%, although they were a small share of all transfers.

The data is older, but it remains the most cited federal estimate, and it lines up with what registrars see: credit that is “accepted” often doesn’t count toward the student’s degree.

Accepted is not the same as applied

A course can transfer and still not help. If an introductory statistics course arrives as a general elective instead of satisfying the major’s math requirement, the student keeps the credit on paper and still has to retake the content. Many credit-loss conversations are really applicability problems, not acceptance problems. That distinction matters for how an office evaluates transcripts:

  1. Evaluate against the program, not just the catalog. Tell the student where each course lands in their intended major.
  2. Write precedent down. When an evaluator decides how a course applies, that decision should become the default the next time.
  3. Answer early. Students compare offers. A credit answer that arrives after the deposit deadline is an answer for a different college.
The transfer gap is built from thousands of small credit decisions. Making those decisions fast, consistent and visible is the part a college controls.

What can registrars and transfer offices do?

  • Measure turnaround time from transcript receipt to a credit answer, by term. Peak-season backlogs are where students drop off.
  • Load state transfer rules as rules. Common course numbering, guaranteed pathways and general education blocks should apply automatically (see state transfer policies across the U.S.).
  • Give students a pre-application answer. A self-service estimate answers “will my credits transfer?” before the student chooses a college.
  • Track credit loss by sending institution to see which partnerships need a new articulation agreement.
  • Close the loop with reverse transfer so students who leave before finishing can still earn the associate degree they qualify for.
